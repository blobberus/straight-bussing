1. The goal: the 10% rule

A prediction made L minutes before a bus arrives should be right to within 10% of L. And predictions should almost never be "too late", the case where a rider misses the bus.

How errors are counted everywhere on this page: error = predicted time minus actual time.

  • Positive = too late: the bus came earlier than shown. The rider can miss it. This is the dangerous side.
  • Negative: the bus came later than shown. The rider waits.

"Too late" uses reading B of your 1% rule: a prediction may be up to 10% of the lead too early, but at most 1% of the lead too late. Two other readings (fewer than 1% of all predictions too late at all, or too late by more than 10%) were checked in E19 and are not reachable with today's spread without wiping out the 10% passes. Please confirm the reading.

Why buses 6+ minutes away matter most

  • People decide when to leave 5 to 15 minutes before the bus. That is the number they act on.
  • Close in, Passio is decent: 2 to 3 minutes ahead, about 6 in 10 predictions are within 1 minute (E18).
  • Far ahead it is not: 10 minutes ahead only about 1 in 7 passes the 10% rule, and the misses all lean one way (the bus comes later than shown). Section 5 has the numbers.

2. How the data is collected

A collector reads Passio's public live feeds every 10 seconds, around the clock, and turns them into files we can score the 10% rule on.

Passio live feedsEvery 10 s: each bus's GPS position, and each bus's predicted arrival at every upcoming stop (the same predictions the apps show), plus alerts.
→
CollectorDetects every real arrival of every bus at every stop, timed to about 10 s, and keeps every prediction update Passio published.
→
arrivals.csvOne row per arrival (25 columns, unchanged): the ground truth.
predictions.csvWhat Passio showed 1, 2, 3, 5, 10, 15, 20 and 30 min before each arrival.
Prediction log + position pollsEvery update and every GPS report, for re-scoring later.
Merge into the data branchOverlapping runs log the same arrival; duplicates (same bus, same stop, within 5 min) are merged, the fullest row and the freshest prediction stay.
→
Daily archive (the morning after)The day's logs and the training table eta-<day>.csv.gz go to the repo's Releases page (about 5.5 MB a day); the pass rates go to data/eta/summary.csv.

Never a gap

  • Cloud runs collect for 70 minutes each and start their successor 40 minutes in, so two always overlap by about 30 minutes.
  • A local backup on this PC collects in 30-minute chunks and reloads the last hour of Passio's predictions at each start, so 30-minute-ahead predictions are never cut off.
How an arrival is detected and timed

The collector watches every bus. An arrival counts when the stop the bus is heading to moves forward along the route (Passio flips it 20 to 50 m early, so the collector waits until the bus stops, turns away, or 60 s pass), or when the bus stands within 40 m of a stop. The time is when the bus's GPS track crosses the stop, worked out between two reports from the bus's speed. Reports come every 10 s and GPS is off by 5 to 10 m, so arrival times are good to about plus or minus 10 seconds. A row is written when the bus leaves, so the time it stood at the stop is known too.

Making sure a prediction belongs to the right arrival (the attribution rules)

Half of the huge "errors" in the old data (E19) were our logger pairing a prediction with the wrong bus or lap, not Passio's fault. The rules now:

  1. Per bus. Several Downtown Campus Connector buses share one trip id, so predictions are kept per bus. One without a bus id counts only when a single bus is on that trip.
  2. Previous lap, same trip id. If the bus already passed this stop on the same trip id, a prediction made before that pass (or for a time within 2 minutes of it) was about the earlier lap and is skipped.
  3. First seen. Passio sometimes changes a predicted time without updating its timestamp (57 of 651 updates one morning, 21 of 8,196 that afternoon). A prediction counts from when our poll first saw it, so a last-minute change never passes for a 10-minute-ahead one.
  4. On display. For "10 minutes ahead" we use the prediction Passio was still showing 10 minutes before the arrival. If Passio had stopped showing it, there is no row: "showed nothing" is counted separately from "showed an old time".

3. How Passio predicts, near and far

Passio does not publish how its predictions work. What follows is what we measured in its feed, plus what other projects found in its app. High Medium Low mark how sure we are.

How the predictions reach the official app and ours

Bus GPSEach bus reports its position to Passio's servers. All prediction math happens there.
→
Passio GO! app and passiogo.comAsk Passio's own ETA service, one stop at a time, about every 10 s. It answers from a cache ("eta cache"), with a live GPS estimate as a fallback.
→
Straight Bussing (web and iPhone)Reads Passio's public feed every 10 s: for each bus, a predicted time at every upcoming stop of its current trip and its next trip. The app shows these times as they are.

Both come from Passio's servers with the same bus and stop ids. When the official app's cached answer was fresh (under about a minute old), it and our feed agreed to about a minute. When the cache was old (we saw 5 to 48 minutes), they could differ a lot, for example 9.3 minutes in the official app against 17.3 in the feed for a 53rd Street Express bus that came after 18.1. In all 13 checks against real arrivals the bus came later than the official app said. 25 checks, one afternoon Medium

How the far predictions are formed High

  1. On the bus's current trip: where the bus is now, plus Passio's own driving times between stops, with no time standing at stops. At 98% of stops Passio predicts the bus leaves 1 second after it arrives, and its stop-to-stop times are about 22 s shorter than the timetable's. So it runs optimistic, and more so the more stops are left.
  2. On the bus's next trip (it still has to finish its current loop): anchored on the timetable. The next trip's first departure equals the scheduled departure in 94% of 232 checks; later stops add Passio's driving times.
  3. Lateness is not carried over. Whether the bus is on time, 1 to 5 minutes late, or more than 5 minutes late, its next-trip predictions stay on the timetable (median miss about 40 to 60 s). Only in 11 of 293 cases did a late bus push its next trip later.
  4. The jump at the end of a loop. When the bus switches to its next trip at the terminal, often while still waiting out its break, the prediction drops the timetable and acts as if the bus leaves right now. Example, bus 4538 at 57th St and Drexel: a steady 3.5 minutes too early until 15 minutes before, then 10 minutes too early at 12 minutes before, shrinking again as the bus came.

What public sources say
  • Passio's own pages and the UChicago transportation pages describe real-time locations and ETAs but not how ETAs are computed. No Passio patent on arrival prediction was found. High
  • Independent open-source projects (MIT, UChicago and Brown student tools) that read passiogo.com describe two paths in Passio's ETA service: a stored "solid" prediction (the normal one) and a live GPS projection along the route (a fallback that is "frequently way off"). Some answers carry the note "got from schedule". Medium
  • passiogo.com's own web code does no prediction math: it formats what the server sends and refreshes an open stop every 10 seconds. High
  • Whether Passio learns driving times from history, uses traffic, or models stops: no source says. Our measurements suggest fixed driving times and no stops. Low

4. How our own model works

Our model is built and tested on the collected data, but the live app does not use it yet: today the app shows Passio's times as they are, and Directions uses the timetable for ride times. It ships only if it passes the ship gate below.

The idea in one line

arrival at stop m = the bus's last real arrival at stop j + the driving time of each stretch from j to m + the time it will stand at each stop in between

A stretch (segment) is the drive between two neighboring stops of a route. Driving time and standing time are learned separately, because traffic drives one and riders drive the other.

Step by step

  1. Clean the history. Leave out stretches longer than 30 minutes (a break or a parked bus), shorter than 3 seconds, or faster than 30 m/s (a GPS glitch), and the logging mistakes found in the E01 audit.
  2. Learn each stretch from coarse to fine. Five levels: all buses, then route by time of day, then the stretch, then the stretch by time of day, then the stretch by hour of the week.
  3. Trust each level only as far as its data allows ("shrinkage"): a level with few buses is pulled toward the level above it; one with hundreds speaks for itself. Medians are used, so one stuck bus cannot move the estimate.
  4. Add the stretches up with honest uncertainty, giving a likely time and an 80% range.
  5. Optionally correct for today (buses running slow this morning). On the data so far this correction does not help, so it stays switched off in practice.
estimate = w x (median of this level) + (1 - w) x (estimate of the level above) w = n / (n + k) n = buses seen at this level, k = 1 on today's data

How it is tested, and when it may ship
  • Time order only: learn on earlier days, test on later days; never mix them, which would leak tomorrow's traffic into the answer.
  • Same information as Passio: for an arrival predicted 10 minutes ahead, the model may only use arrivals logged before that moment.
  • Ship gate (measured on at least 4 weeks): for times shown to riders, beat Passio at 2 to 5 and 5 to 10 minutes ahead on at least 10 test days, never make "the bus came early" more common than Passio does, and no route more than 10% worse. For Directions ride times, beat the timetable by 20% with honest 80% ranges.
  • A position filter (Kalman) was tried in simulation only: it cleans GPS, but its speed is useless for arrival times (a bus at a stop has speed 0).
Where the numbers in the app come from today
  • "Bus in N min": Passio's predicted time for that stop from the live feed, including the next trip's bus, which is where far buses (10, 20, 30 minutes) come from. Whole minutes, rounded down. Not adjusted.
  • Honesty labels: no successful update for 60 s shows an error; feed data over 2 minutes old is marked delayed; a feed outage is never shown as "no shuttles running".
  • Directions: walking from distance (refined with sidewalk routes); waiting from the next live bus you can reach; riding from Passio's own times for the same bus, else the timetable. Every derived number says "est.".
  • Current trip and the Lock Screen: only the planned bus's live Passio times; "data delayed" after 2 minutes without news.

6. What we train next

The collector now builds a training table every day, shaped around the moment a rider asks.

The training table: eta-<day>.csv.gz

  • One row: one minute, one bus, and one stop that bus really reached within the next 45 minutes. About 130,000 rows a day.
  • What was known at that minute: route, trip, whether the target is on the bus's next trip, stops and meters to go, the last stop it reached and how long ago, its speed and how long it has stood still, how late it runs against the timetable, time of day, weekday or weekend, the school calendar.
  • What each source predicted: Passio (or "showed nothing"), the timetable, and the timetable plus delay.
  • The answer: the real arrival, the error, and the error as a share of the lead. That share makes the 10% rule one number at every lead: between -0.10 and +0.10 passes; above +0.01 is "too late".

How it is judged: one sample per arrival in each range (0 to 6, 6 to 10, 10 to 15, 15 to 20, 20 to 30, 30 to 45 minutes), never an average over rows, with the uncertainty taken over days. A verdict needs at least 10 days.

What should fix the far-ahead errors

  1. Correct Passio where it is wrong in a predictable way: by whether the target is on the current or next trip, stops left, break time left at the terminal, route and hour. Its error is one-sided and structural, so there is a lot of room.
  2. Next trips from the timetable, done right: start at the scheduled departure, but later if the bus will reach the terminal later; then add learned driving and standing times.
  3. A terminal break model (when does a bus really leave) and run matching (which scheduled run a bus is really on).
  4. A standing-bus rule for buses that have not moved for minutes.
  5. Keep "too late" under 1% of the lead while improving: any correction is checked against the bus-came-early rate first.

First look at Passio far ahead with a week of data: around Oct 19, 2026. Decision with at least 10 days: around Oct 22.

7. Glossary

Lead
How long before the arrival a prediction was shown. "10 minutes ahead" = a lead of 10 minutes.
Error
Predicted time minus actual time. Positive = too late (the bus came early); negative = the bus came later than shown.
10% rule
The miss is at most 10% of the lead. With a 1-minute floor, never stricter than 1 minute.
Too late (reading B)
Predicted later than the bus came by more than 1% of the lead.
Huge (gross) miss
Off by more than 10 minutes, or half the lead if that is more. Left out of the pass rate, always counted, and a gross early bus still counts against "too late".
Current trip / next trip
The run the bus is driving now / the run it starts after finishing this one, usually after a break at the terminal.
Layover (break)
A bus waiting at the end of its route before its next run.
Trip id
Passio's name for one scheduled run. It can flip mid-run, repeat every day, or be shared by several buses.
Run matching
Working out which scheduled run a bus is really on; the trip id is often an earlier one.
Stretch (segment)
The drive between two neighboring stops of a route.
Shrinkage
Pulling an estimate from little data toward a broader one.
Stale
A prediction Passio has not refreshed; also the app's "data delayed" state.
